                  Where the deaths are

                    About the data and method

                    Events. Each point is one incident of organized violence with at least one death, coded by the Uppsala Conflict Data Program. 2024–2025 come from UCDP GED Global 26.1. January–August 2026 come from the monthly UCDP Candidate releases, which are preliminary and can change. Deaths use UCDP’s best estimate.

                    Three kinds of violence. State-based conflict involves a government. Non-state conflict is fighting between organized armed groups. One-sided violence is the deliberate killing of civilians by a government or armed group.

                    Heat. A Gaussian kernel density on the sphere, computed in your browser from the events that match the filters. It is annualized, so a one-month window and a twelve-month window share the same colour scale. The scale is logarithmic: each step of colour is ten times more deaths.

                    Country-level totals. UCDP records some deaths only by country, mostly in monthly totals for Ukraine and in the January 2026 crackdown in Iran. These do not appear as dots. With “Place country-level deaths” on, each such total is shared among the same conflict’s located events in proportion to their deaths.

                    Conflict footprint. Following the 50 km convention used in PRIO’s estimates of people living in conflict zones, every 0.25° grid cell (about 28 km across at the equator) whose centre lies within 50 km of a located event in the window counts as a conflict zone. Area and population are summed over those cells. Population comes from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHS-POP R2023A, 2025 epoch), aggregated from its 1 km grid. Inhabited land means cells averaging at least one person per km² of land, which covers 44% of land and nearly all people. Deaths recorded only by country are not included, because they have no location.

                    Map. The globe is a true-proportion model of Earth (mean radius 6,371 km). Altitude and the scale bar are in real kilometres. Imagery: NASA Blue Marble Next Generation, July 2004. Boundaries: Natural Earth 1:50m, with Crimea shown as part of Ukraine to match UCDP’s coding. Gaza and the West Bank are listed separately from Israel.

                    Detail up close. Below about 5,000 km altitude the ground is drawn from map tiles that sharpen as you zoom in, down to about 2.5 km from the ground. Imagery: EOxCloudless 2025 by EOX IT Services GmbH (Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2025), a cloud-free mosaic of Sentinel-2 images with 10 m pixels, licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; the globe lights and recolours it like the rest of the map. Streets, street names and place names come from OpenStreetMap contributors as OpenMapTiles vector tiles served by OpenFreeMap, drawn in your browser. Names are in English where OpenStreetMap has an English name, and otherwise in Latin letters or the local script.

                    Sky and terrain. The globe is held in Earth’s own frame, and the Sun, Moon, planets and stars move around it as Earth turns, so day and night, moonlight and eclipse shadows fall where they really are. Positions come from astronomy-engine (Don Cross, MIT licence), accurate to about an arcminute. The Moon is drawn at its true size and distance. During a solar eclipse each point on the ground is dimmed by the share of the Sun’s disc that the Moon covers as seen from there, drawn darker than the eye would see so that partial phases show, and a thin line marks where the partial eclipse ends. Relief comes from NOAA ETOPO 2022 (60 arc-second surface elevation), averaged to 0.05° (about 5.5 km) and raised around the view below 4,500 km altitude. It is at true scale by default, with 2× and 4× exaggeration available. Night lights: NASA Black Marble 2016. Moon surface: NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter.

                    Clouds. Derived from NOAA/NESDIS GMGSI, the Global Mosaic of Geostationary Satellite Imagery, through NOAA Open Data Dissemination on AWS: hourly longwave infrared at about 8 km from GOES (NOAA), Meteosat (EUMETSAT) and Himawari (JMA). Processed, not original NOAA data; contains modified EUMETSAT Meteosat data. Each hour’s brightness temperatures become cloud opacity, clear at about 288 K near the equator (263 K at 72°) and opaque at 225 K and colder. The mosaic stops at 72.7° north and south.

                    Light pollution. Artificial zenith sky brightness from Falchi, F. et al. (2016), The new world atlas of artificial night sky brightness, Science Advances 2(6):e1600377, and its data supplement (GFZ Data Services, doi:10.5880/GFZ.1.4.2016.001, CC BY-NC 4.0), modelled from 2014 VIIRS satellite data. It covers 85° N to 60° S. Brightness is averaged into 0.05° cells and added to a natural sky of 22.0 mag/arcsec². Bortle classes use these upper limits of total zenith brightness: 1 above 21.99, 2 above 21.89, 3 above 21.69, 4 above 20.49, 5 above 19.50, 6 above 18.94, 7 above 18.38, and 8–9 brighter. Light pollution has grown since 2014 in many places, so present-day skies are often brighter than shown.

                    Trafficking flows. Counter-Trafficking Data Collaborative (CTDC), Global Synthetic Dataset, 2026 release (IOM, with case data from Polaris, A21, RecollectiV and the Portuguese Observatory on Trafficking in Human Beings), ctdatacollaborative.org. The records are synthesised with differential privacy, so they keep the statistics of the case records without describing real people. A flow counts the records whose citizenship and country of exploitation are both known and differ, pooled over 2002–2024, and pairs with fewer than 100 records are left out because synthetic counts that small are unreliable. Arcs join each country’s population-weighted centre (GHSL 2025). The data are not a random sample: they cover people identified where the contributors work, and CTDC notes that this identification bias cannot be measured or corrected. Source: Counter-Trafficking Data Collaborative (CTDC), October 2026. North America’s domestic share: UNODC, Global Report on Trafficking in Persons 2024, p. 133.

                    Named highways. U.S. interstates that a state official, law-enforcement agency, anti-trafficking organisation or news report has publicly called a human trafficking corridor or route, collected by hand with the exact words, date and link for each, and checked on the source pages. Naming is not measurement: trafficking cases occur along most interstates, and no dataset ranks highways by trafficking. Where a source speaks of one state, the card says so, but the whole interstate is drawn. Routes: Natural Earth 10m roads (public domain).

                    Progress since 1990 (Good view). Under-5 mortality, the share of children who die before their fifth birthday, from the UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME), and period life expectancy at birth from the UN World Population Prospects (UN WPP), both through Our World in Data (CC BY 4.0), for every year from 1990 to the latest; the year bar plays them one year at a time. Countries are coloured by class, either by level or by change since 1990 (the fall in the under-5 death rate relative to 1990, or the years of life expectancy gained). Each of the six classes holds about a sixth of countries in the latest year, and the same class edges apply to every year, so playing the years shows countries moving up through them. Countries worse off than in 1990 in the year shown have their own grey-violet colour. The classes are shown as light, brighter and greener with more progress: gentle in daylight and, on the night side, gathered around where people live, shaped by a blurred copy of NASA’s Black Marble night lights; its colour and strength come only from the country’s class. These are national averages that hide inequality between regions and groups, and the latest estimates, often modelled, can hide recent setbacks from war, epidemics or economic crises.

                    A sense of scale (Good view). The number of children who died before their fifth birthday worldwide in 1990 and in the latest year (UN IGME via Our World in Data, CC BY 4.0), drawn to the same scale as the deaths in organized armed violence that UCDP recorded in the months chosen in the conflict view. They measure different things, so the comparison gives a sense of scale; it is not good minus bad.

                    Peace agreements (Good view). Every agreement since 2020 in PA-X, the Peace Agreements Database (version 10, PeaceRep, University of Edinburgh, peaceagreements.org), placed at the population-weighted centre (GHSL 2025) of each country PA-X names for it. PA-X collects ceasefires, pre-negotiation and framework agreements, implementation deals and renewals, between states and within them, including local ones. Many are partial, cover one region or armed group, or are later broken, so a signature marks a step, not a settled peace. PA-X is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, and the agreement data on this page (data/peace.json) is shared under the same licence.

                    Good news now (Good view, experimental). GDELT 2.0 events from the last 72 hours, refreshed every 3 hours, that GDELT codes as material cooperation (economic or judicial cooperation, aid, eased restrictions or sanctions, released people, de-escalation) or the signing of a formal agreement, in source articles with an average tone of at least 2. Military cooperation and military aid are left out. Each article counts once, at its most precise location, and listings, travel, property, sport and articles whose web address names attacks, killing, war, bombs, shootings, crashes or deaths are dropped. The sorting is automatic, so the layer may include unrelated stories.

                    Cite. Sundberg & Melander (2013), Introducing the UCDP Georeferenced Event Dataset, Journal of Peace Research 50(4). Hegre et al. (2020), Introducing the UCDP Candidate Events Dataset, Research & Politics. UCDP data are licensed CC BY 4.0. Population: Schiavina, Melchiorri & Pesaresi (2023), GHS-POP R2023A, European Commission Joint Research Centre, CC BY 4.0.
